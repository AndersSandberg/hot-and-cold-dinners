# SSP3-7.0 and the UK food basket in 2100

## A source-backed evidence dossier for ingredient-level scenario work

**Prepared:** 19 August 2026  
**Geographic focus:** United Kingdom  
**Time horizon:** late century, principally 2081–2100  

## Executive finding

SSP3-7.0 creates a striking divergence between **what Britain could grow climatically** and **what its food system would be able to supply reliably**.

- The physical climate probably expands the UK’s technically suitable crop basket. A late-century warming near 4°C makes maize, broad beans, potatoes, pears and sugar beet more climatically suitable across much of the country, and opens substantial potential for sorghum, durum wheat, safflower, soy, chickpea, cowpea, sesame, grapes and several warmer-climate fruits.
- That opportunity is geographically uneven. Northern and western Britain gain most; the present arable heartlands of eastern and south-eastern England gain least and face the strongest summer water stress. Wheat remains viable nationally in the crop-suitability model, but its suitability shifts northwards; oats, apples, onions and strawberries show regional losses.
- The SSP3 socioeconomic storyline works in the opposite direction. Trade barriers, low investment, weak agricultural research, deteriorating machinery and infrastructure, declining access to fertiliser and pesticides, and later institutional fragmentation suppress the ability to turn climatic suitability into production.
- The likely **realised** 2100 diet is therefore not simply “Mediterranean Britain.” It is more plausibly a narrower, seasonal and regional diet built around grains, roots, pulses and hardy vegetables, with intermittent animal products and much less reliable access to tropical beverages, cocoa, rice, bananas, citrus, spices, vegetable oils and imported feed.

This conclusion is conditional. SSP3-7.0 is a scenario, not a forecast, and it does not specify individual crops. Ingredient outcomes have to be assembled from several model families whose assumptions are not fully consistent.

## 1. What SSP3-7.0 does—and does not—specify

SSP3-7.0 combines two different things:

1. **SSP3, “Regional Rivalry”**: a socioeconomic pathway with resurgent nationalism, low international cooperation, slow technological progress, weak investment in education and health, high inequality, material-intensive consumption and strong pressures on land and environmental systems.
2. **A 7.0 W/m² forcing pathway in 2100**: a high-emissions climate trajectory used in CMIP6 and the IPCC Sixth Assessment.

It is an illustrative pathway, not the IPCC’s prediction of 2100. The IPCC gives a best estimate of **3.6°C global warming in 2081–2100 relative to 1850–1900**, with a very likely range of **2.8–4.6°C**. The mid-century best estimate is 2.1°C (1.7–2.6°C). Source: [IPCC AR6 WGI Summary for Policymakers, Table SPM.1](https://d30mzt1bxg5llt.cloudfront.net/public/uploads/PDFs/IPCC_AR6_WGI_SPM.pdf).

SSP3-7.0 itself does **not** state that Britain grows a particular crop, that a given import disappears, or that a particular government collapses. Those are downstream results or narrative elaborations. This dossier therefore labels claims by evidence type:

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **D — Direct** | An SSP3/SSP3-7.0 assumption or IPCC climate result |
| **U — UK extension** | A result from the official UK-SSP3 national storyline; compatible with SSP3 but not mandated by the global scenario |
| **M — Modelled impact** | An output from a crop, land-use, food-trade or marine impact model |
| **I — Inference** | An ingredient-level interpretation made by joining those results |

## 3. Britain’s physical growing environment

There is no single canonical “UK SSP3-7.0 weather file.” UKCP18 primarily uses RCPs and global-warming levels, while CMIP6 uses SSP-labelled experiments. A **+4°C global-warming level** is the best available UK crop-impact analogue for SSP3-7.0’s 3.6°C late-century central estimate, although it sits above that median and within its likely range.

The Met Office’s [UKCP18](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/approach/collaboration/ukcp) remains the principal national projection set, with global, regional and local products. The crop study used here applies UKCP18/CHESS-SCAPE projections at +2°C and +4°C.

### Robust physical tendencies

| Variable | Late-century UK tendency | Relevance to ingredients | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growing-season warmth | At +4°C, UK growing degree days rise about 60% relative to the lower-warming reference used in a UK indicators study | Enables maize, grapes, durum wheat and several pulses/oilseeds farther north | High for direction |
| Frost | Days below 0°C fall substantially; the +1.5°C to +4°C comparison gives a reduction of up to 49 days/year | Fewer frost losses and broader perennial-fruit options, but poorer winter-chill conditions for some cultivars | High for direction |
| Hot days | Up to roughly 39 additional hot days/year between +1.5°C and +4°C warming levels | Heat damage around flowering/grain filling; livestock heat stress; higher cooling need | High |
| Summer water balance | Drier summers and more severe soil-moisture drought, especially in southern and eastern England | Irrigation becomes decisive for horticulture, potatoes and some high-value crops | High |
| Winter precipitation | Generally wetter winters and heavier rainfall events | Waterlogging, erosion, delayed sowing and harvesting, nutrient runoff | High |
| Spatial centre of advantage | Climatic gains are generally larger in Scotland, Wales, western and northern England than in the south-east | Production and processing infrastructure would need to migrate or be rebuilt | Medium–high |
| CO₂ fertilisation | Potential yield benefit for some C3 crops, limited by water, nutrients, ozone, heat and pests | Should not be counted as a guaranteed yield gain; may also reduce grain nutrient density | Medium/contested |

The quantified indicator ranges are from [Hanlon et al., *Climate Change* (2021)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10584-021-03100-5). They compare global-warming levels rather than provide an exact SSP3-7.0 time slice, so they are treated as an analogue.

### Consequences not captured by simple suitability maps

- **Water becomes a binding input.** A crop can be thermally suitable while being commercially impossible without irrigation. This is especially important in eastern and south-eastern England.
- **Extreme events dominate annual means.** A warmer average growing season does not prevent a single heatwave, wet harvest or spring drought from destroying a crop.
- **Pests and diseases move.** Warmer winters increase overwinter survival, while weak surveillance and reduced pesticide access in SSP3 make biological threats more consequential.
- **Cultivar chill requirements matter.** “Apple” or “pear” is not one climatic entity; existing cultivars may fail even when the crop category remains suitable.
- **Soil degradation can cancel climatic gains.** The UK-SSP3 storyline explicitly expects declining natural capital and later land degradation.

Several cautions matter especially for the exotic fruit entries:

- A rising suitability index is not proof of consistent commercial harvests.
- EcoCrop uses broad crop thresholds and can miss flowering phenology, chill requirements, wind exposure, novel pests and soil constraints.
- A viable orchard planted in 2080 requires nursery stock, breeding, investment and confidence decades earlier—conditions that the SSP3 storyline undermines.
- Protected cropping, glasshouses and vertical agriculture were excluded. In an energy- and capital-poor SSP3 Britain, their scale would probably be limited unless attached to a reliable local heat or power source.

## 6. Land use and domestic production under a UK-SSP3 analogue

Brown et al. modelled UK land use to 2080 under the national SSP narratives. Its UK-SSP3 run is paired with **RCP6.0, not SSP3-7.0**, so it is a socioeconomic analogue with a somewhat lower climate forcing than the scenario requested.

The model produces:

- an extensive, food-dominated landscape;
- conversion of some forest to arable production;
- a shift from intensive pastoral production toward extensive arable systems;
- low capital and agricultural inputs;
- major divergence among England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland after institutional fragmentation; and
- crop supply reaching only about **60% of demand at some points** in the run.

That 60% is not a 2100 forecast and should not be treated as one. It is evidence that more land does not compensate automatically for low yields, limited inputs and institutional breakdown.

Source: [Brown et al., “Exploring the future of land use and food security: a new set of UK scenarios,” open manuscript (2022)](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/533621/1/N533621JA.pdf).

## 7. Imports: the decisive ambiguity

### Present dependence

Recent UK food-security statistics provide the starting point:

- The UK produced about **65% of all food consumed** and **77% of foods indigenous to the UK** in 2024.
- Domestic production supplied roughly **53% of vegetables** and only **15–17% of fruit**.
- In 2023, **64% of food imports by volume came from the EU**.
- The short Channel crossings handled 18% of total food, feed and drink imports, with much higher shares for perishable dairy/eggs, meat, fruit and vegetables.

Sources: [UK Government Food Strategy](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/a-uk-government-food-strategy-for-england/a-uk-government-food-strategy-for-england-considering-the-wider-uk-food-system), [UK Food Security Report 2024—supply sources](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/united-kingdom-food-security-report-2024/united-kingdom-food-security-report-2024-theme-2-uk-food-supply-sources), and [supply-chain resilience](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/united-kingdom-food-security-report-2024/united-kingdom-food-security-report-2024-theme-3-food-supply-chain-resilience).

### Two incompatible but useful futures

| Case | Assumption | Implication for ingredients |
|---|---|---|
| **Narrative-consistent Regional Rivalry** | UK-SSP3’s closed borders, declining trade, fragmented governance and deteriorating distribution | Imported foods become costly, intermittent or absent. Domestic climatic suitability and local processing capacity dominate the diet. |
| **World market continues to function** | A 2026 LandSyMM SSP3-RCP7.0 study keeps present trade preferences and a single world commodity market | Imports continue, and the UK’s overseas agricultural land footprint remains large; ingredient diversity is much greater than in the strict SSP3 narrative. |

The LandSyMM study is valuable as a counterfactual, but it cannot answer Regional Rivalry availability directly because it does not model bilateral trade disruption. It estimates that the UK’s 2020 imports embodied **10.8 million hectares** of overseas agricultural land, compared with 16.8 million hectares of UK agricultural area. Soybeans, wheat, cocoa, maize and rapeseed represented 55% of the import cropland footprint. Across its scenarios the 2100 import footprint remains around 12.8 million hectares at the median; in its SSP3-RCP7.0 run, food-import cropland falls about 10% by 2100 while some animal-product imports rise earlier. Source: [PLOS ONE, “The global land footprint of UK food imports under future scenarios” (2026)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0352499).

For a paper explicitly about **Regional Rivalry**, the first case should be the main storyline and the second a sensitivity case.

### Ingredients most exposed to failed trade

| Ingredient group | UK replacement potential | Likely strict-SSP3 outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Bananas and tropical fruit | Low outdoors | Rare luxury or absent; some replacement by pears, apples, berries, grapes, quince and possibly new orchard fruits |

| Vegetable oils | Rapeseed already domestic; safflower, sunflower, flax, hemp, sesame and soy become options | Oil supply can be domestic but remains land-, machinery- and processing-intensive; animal fat may regain importance locally |

## 8. Livestock, dairy and eggs

The crop evidence alone would overstate animal-food availability. UK-SSP3’s initial response to import restriction is likely intensification and pressure on domestic feed production, followed by declining access to machinery, veterinary inputs and concentrated feed. A recent UK livestock-scenario analysis describes a mid- to late-century transition toward fractured, low-input subsistence systems, with higher disease and social risks.

Likely ingredient consequences:

- **Ruminant meat declines but persists regionally.** Grassland in wetter western and upland areas remains a resource that humans cannot eat directly, so sheep and cattle do not vanish. Meat becomes less frequent and more seasonal.
- **Dairy becomes geographically concentrated.** Milk, butter and cheese persist where grass, water, breeding and cold chains remain reliable. Fermented and aged products are more robust than fresh milk in a weak distribution system.
- **Pigs and poultry are highly feed-sensitive.** They convert human-edible cereals and imported protein feeds; eggs and pork/chicken may contract sharply when soy and grain are scarce, although household scraps and by-products support small systems.
- **Heat and disease reduce reliability.** Heat stress, parasite expansion and weaker veterinary surveillance suppress productivity.
- **Offal, blood, bones, whey and rendered fats gain value.** SSP3’s low-waste logic makes whole-animal and by-product use more likely.

These are robust directional inferences, not quantified 2100 headcounts.

## 9. Fish and seafood

Marine warming redistributes rather than simply eliminates UK seafood. High-emissions projections indicate substantial losses for several familiar North Sea species by 2100, while warm-water species expand northwards.

| Tendency | Candidate ingredients | Important qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Declining or shifting north | Cod, haddock, herring; some salmonid resources | Individual stocks differ; fishing pressure and management can dominate climate signal |
| Increasing access around UK waters | Sardines, anchovies, sea bass, red mullet, squid/octopus and occasional tuna | New fleets, gear, markets and processing are needed |
| Acidification/heat risk | Mussels, oysters and other calcifying shellfish | Disease and harmful blooms compound direct shell effects |
| Governance risk | Shared and migratory stocks | SSP3’s weak international cooperation makes quota conflict and overfishing more likely |

A high-emissions European fisheries analysis reports late-century North Sea declines of roughly 10–80% for cod, haddock and herring, depending on species and model. The broad direction is supported by the [Marine Climate Change Impacts Partnership fisheries review](https://www.mccip.org.uk/fisheries); a recent synthesis is summarised by [Plymouth Marine Laboratory](https://pml.ac.uk/news/climate-change-to-reshape-european-fisheries-new-study-maps-winners-and-losers/). These figures should not be summed or treated as a forecast of UK landings.

### Likely scarce, irregular or luxury ingredients

- coffee, cocoa and conventional tea;
- bananas, pineapples, mangoes and most tropical fruit;
- rice and many imported noodles unless exchange with suitable regions persists;
- pepper, cinnamon, vanilla and most tropical spices;
- olive, palm and coconut oils;
- out-of-season fresh salad vegetables and berries;
- ocean fish tied to declining cold-water stocks;
- meat, eggs and farmed fish dependent on imported soy or grain feed;
- highly processed foods whose additives, packaging, refrigeration and multi-stage supply chains span borders.

## 11. A useful two-axis framing for the paper

Ingredient availability in 2100 should be modelled on two separate axes:

| Axis | Low end | High end |
|---|---|---|
| **Biophysical suitability** | Crop cannot mature or survive reliably in the UK climate | Crop is rain-fed, resilient and agronomically well matched |
| **System capability** | No seed, inputs, labour, machinery, processing, storage or trade | Strong institutions and complete production chain |

This prevents a common category error. Soy or mandarin may move strongly upward on climatic suitability while remaining low on system capability in SSP3. Conversely, oats may gain little climatically but remain highly available because Britain already has seed, knowledge, mills and food traditions for them.

For scenario design, ingredients can be placed in four cells:

1. **Suitable + capable:** the dependable core basket.
2. **Suitable + incapable:** latent crops that require deliberate pre-adaptation.
3. **Unsuitable + trade-accessible:** imports, viable only in the functioning-market sensitivity case.
4. **Unsuitable + trade-inaccessible:** absent or substituted ingredients.

## 12. What can be stated with confidence

### Strong conclusions

- SSP3-7.0 gives a late-century warming level close enough to +4°C for existing UK +4°C crop studies to be a defensible central analogue.
- The UK’s thermal growing season expands, while summer drought, heat extremes, wet-winter damage and year-to-year volatility also intensify.
- The climatic crop basket becomes broader and shifts north and west.
- Pulses, warmer-season grains and alternative oilseeds are among the most credible expansion opportunities.
- Strict Regional Rivalry makes trade, inputs, processing and governance more constraining than aggregate climatic suitability.
- Present UK dependence on imported fruit, vegetables, feed and tropical foods makes ingredient diversity particularly vulnerable.

### Findings that should remain conditional

- Exact 2100 UK production tonnage or self-sufficiency: no coherent model found combines SSP3 trade barriers, SSP3-7.0 climate, crop adaptation, water, pests, processing and political fragmentation.
- Exact national population: the official UK-SSP3 downscaling has flat-then-declining qualitative trends, while some global-model implementations assume rapid UK population growth.
- Commercial viability of exotic fruits: broad climate-envelope scores are not orchard trials.
- Fisheries quantities: stock management and international cooperation can reverse or amplify the climate signal.
- The UK-SSP3 collapse narrative: it is a deliberately challenging national scenario extension, not a prediction compelled by the IPCC.

## 13. Data needed for a quantitative next stage

A defensible ingredient model would need to add:

1. UKCP18/CMIP6 gridded monthly climate for several SSP3-7.0 ensemble members, not just the +4°C median analogue.
2. Crop-specific yield models including irrigation, heat at flowering, CO₂ response, pests, soils and cultivar adaptation.
3. Water availability by catchment and season.
4. Agricultural land allocation with explicit human-food versus feed competition.
5. Seed, fertiliser, machinery, fuel, labour, storage and processing constraints.
6. Two explicit trade regimes: Regional Rivalry and a functioning-world-market sensitivity.
7. Dietary requirements, not just crop tonnage: calories, protein quality, fats, vitamins B12/C/D, iron, zinc and iodine.
8. A shock layer for compound droughts, floods, animal disease, marine heatwaves and simultaneous import failures.

The appropriate output would be a probabilistic ingredient table with fields for crop, region, season, yield range, water requirement, storage life, processing dependence, import exposure, nutrition and confidence—rather than one deterministic “2100 menu.”

## 15. Recommended use in the paper

Use this evidence as a **constraint set**, not as a single forecast. A transparent paper should present at least two late-century UK food cases:

- **SSP3-consistent constrained case:** trade and institutions degrade; the basket is domestic, seasonal and limited by inputs and processing.
- **Climate-only/functional-trade sensitivity:** the same physical climate, but trade and technical adaptation continue; the UK grows a wider range of crops and retains imported ingredient diversity.

The contrast between them identifies the main result: in SSP3-7.0, institutional capability and international exchange are likely to determine what is eaten more strongly than the direct climatic limits on what can grow.
