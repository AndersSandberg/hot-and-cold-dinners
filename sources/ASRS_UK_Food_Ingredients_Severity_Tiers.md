# Abrupt sunlight reduction and the UK food basket

## A source-backed evidence dossier organised from Baseline to Severe

**Prepared:** 19 August 2026  
**Geographic focus:** United Kingdom  
**Event horizon:** the first 15 years after an abrupt sunlight reduction scenario (ASRS)

## Executive finding

An abrupt sunlight reduction is not a single forecast. It is a family of shocks—principally nuclear-war smoke, volcanic sulfate and impact dust—that reduce surface light and temperature over very different durations and with different accompanying damage. The most usable published severity ladder comes from six nuclear-war simulations with **5, 16, 27, 37, 47 and 150 teragrams (Tg) of stratospheric soot**. This dossier groups them as:

- **Baseline:** control climate, 0 Tg soot;
- **Mild:** 5 Tg;
- **Moderate:** 16–47 Tg, retaining the individual values rather than pretending this broad range is homogeneous; and
- **Severe:** 150 Tg, the published US–Russia nuclear-winter benchmark.

For Britain, the implications are strongly non-linear:

- At **5 Tg**, one model gives a small *increase* in UK domestic food calories in Year 2, but this is not a food-security forecast. It is relative to domestic 2013 production, assumes international trade has stopped, simplifies livestock adjustment and does not test whether production meets the UK population's absolute calorie requirement. Imports of fruit, vegetables, oils, rice, soy and tropical ingredients could still fall sharply.
- Across **16–47 Tg**, the same model gives UK Year-2 domestic calorie losses of roughly **33–79%** under its livestock case. At the central 27 Tg anchor, the loss is **43.8%**, or **35.8%** if livestock is removed and half of food-competing feed is redirected to people. Roots, small grains, oilseed rape, sugar beet, hardy brassicas and pulses become the most plausible outdoor ingredients, but no crop is reliable without testing under low light and frost.
- At **150 Tg**, modelled UK conventional food output falls **95.8–98.7%** in Year 2. Conventional outdoor agriculture is then a residual rather than the core food system. Survival depends on stored food, rapid livestock slaughter, rationing, functioning energy and industry, and large-scale production or import of foods such as lignocellulosic sugar and microbial protein. Those technologies have promising engineering studies but have not been demonstrated at the required emergency scale.

The decisive conclusion is that **forcing severity and response capability are separate axes**. A severe climate shock with intact industry, trade and preparation can outperform a moderate shock accompanied by war damage, export bans, energy failure and poor distribution. A paper should therefore cross the four physical tiers with at least two response cases: **unprepared/fragmented** and **prepared/cooperative**.

## 1. Scope, terminology and evidence labels

### ASRS is an umbrella term

This dossier uses ASRS for a sudden, sustained reduction in sunlight sufficient to disrupt agriculture. The main triggers are:

1. smoke from mass fires after nuclear war;
2. sulfate aerosols and ash from a very large volcanic eruption; and
3. dust, sulfate and soot from an asteroid or comet impact.

The mechanisms are not interchangeable. Nuclear smoke absorbs sunlight, self-lofts and can remain in the stratosphere for years; a nuclear event also brings blast, fire, fallout, ozone loss and probable infrastructure damage. Volcanic sulfate normally has a shorter atmospheric lifetime and does not imply war damage. Impact dust has different particle physics and may be accompanied by fires, tsunamis and regional devastation. **A soot mass cannot be translated directly into an equivalent mass of sulfate or impact dust.** Cross-hazard comparisons should instead use surface shortwave reduction, temperature, precipitation, seasonality and duration.

### Evidence labels

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **D — Direct scenario output** | A value reported by a climate, crop, fishery or food-system model for a specified forcing |
| **B — Baseline observation** | Contemporary UK production, land-use or import evidence |
| **A — Adaptation model** | An engineering, optimisation or scale-up estimate for an intervention |
| **I — Inference** | The UK ingredient interpretation made by joining published results |
| **E — Emerging evidence** | Preprint, laboratory pilot or result not yet independently replicated |

Most severe-ASRS evidence is modelled rather than observed. The 2025 US National Academies review finds important uncertainty at every link from weapons employment, urban fuel and fire spread through plume lofting, aerosol properties, climate response and ecosystem and societal impacts. The soot quantity should therefore be treated as a **conditional forcing**, not as a precise prediction from a stated number of warheads. Source: [National Academies, *Potential Environmental Effects of Nuclear War* (2025)](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/27515/potential-environmental-effects-of-nuclear-war).

## 2. A practical four-tier framework

The published nuclear-war literature contains six soot cases rather than four named tiers. The grouping below is an analytical convenience for this paper, not an official ALLFED or IPCC classification.

| Tier | Published forcing used here | Physical interpretation | Food-system interpretation |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **Baseline** | 0 Tg/control | Normal interannual climate variability | Present food system; optionally add a separate trade-disruption control |
| **Mild** | 5 Tg soot | Severe global anomaly, but conventional crops remain important | Rationing, feed diversion, cultivar change and trade policy dominate outcome |
| **Moderate** | 16, 27, 37 and 47 Tg | Multi-year cooling and dimming; large mid/high-latitude crop losses | Conventional farming contracts sharply; resilient foods begin to determine survival |
| **Severe** | 150 Tg | Decadal nuclear-winter benchmark, with the first 2–4 years most extreme | Conventional UK agriculture nearly ceases; industrial and imported resilient foods are essential |

“Mild” is only relative to the larger experiments. The 5 Tg simulation produces a global crop shock outside the FAO-era historical range and should not be described as mild in ordinary policy language.

### Recommended time phases

| Phase | Approximate timing | Dominant foods and constraints |
|---|---|---|
| **Immediate** | Event to 3 months | Stocks already in shops, warehouses and homes; fresh livestock products; disruption of transport, power and payment systems |
| **Bridging** | 3–12 months | Rationed grain, oil and sugar stocks; livestock slaughter; preserved fish; first altered sowings; emergency imports if trade functions |
| **Trough** | Years 1–3, sometimes through Year 5 | Lowest conventional output; feed and pasture failure; strongest need for crop relocation, greenhouses and industrial foods |
| **Partial recovery** | Years 3–10 | Gradual recovery of light and temperature; depleted soils, herds, seed stocks and machinery may lag the climate |
| **Long tail** | Years 10–15+ | Climate closer to baseline, but ozone, ocean, demographic and institutional effects can persist |

The timing is not fixed. The principal nuclear simulations inject smoke on 15 May, so their crop losses contain a particular seasonal assumption. A winter event would interact differently with stocks, planting dates and standing crops.

## 3. Canonical physical and production data

Xia et al. combine CESM-WACCM climate simulations with the CLM5 crop model, marine productivity and fishery models, and stylised livestock cases. Their six scenarios are the most complete common spine for the tier system. The climate effects peak during the first years and persist for roughly a decade.

| Soot case | Tier | Reported global/crop-region physical signal | Global conventional-food signal |
|---:|---|---|---|
| **0 Tg** | Baseline | Control climatology | Control production |
| **5 Tg** | Mild | Maximum crop-region cooling about **1.5°C**; crop-region downwelling solar loss about **10 W/m²**. A separate multi-model study gives global mean cooling **1.8°C** and precipitation **−7.9%**, averaged over the first five years. | Four simulated crops average **−7% calories** in Years 1–5 in CLM5; a six-crop-model comparison gives larger losses, showing CLM5 is conservative. Ocean net primary production (NPP) falls about **3%**. |
| **16 Tg** | Moderate—lower | Literature synthesis associates it with roughly **2.5°C** global cooling; exact spatial and seasonal changes are much larger over northern continental cropland. | Large but geographically uneven food loss; the UK-specific values below are more informative than a global average. |
| **27 Tg** | Moderate—central | Literature synthesis associates it with roughly **4.5°C** global cooling. | High-latitude Northern Hemisphere crop calorie losses commonly exceed **50%**; fish catch in affected high-latitude waters falls roughly **20–30%**. |
| **37 Tg** | Moderate—upper | Literature synthesis associates it with roughly **5.5°C** global cooling. | Used in the 2025 food-trade model: most countries lose **50–100%** of imports of wheat, maize, rice and soy because major exporters are badly affected. |
| **47 Tg** | Moderate—upper | Literature synthesis associates it with roughly **6.5°C** global cooling. | Four-crop calories average about **−50%** in Years 1–5. Trade roles change markedly around this scale in the network study. |
| **150 Tg** | Severe | Maximum crop-region cooling **14.8°C**; very large solar and precipitation loss. One global analysis gives almost **10°C** global mean monthly cooling in the first 2–3 years; an integrated food model uses Year-2 cropland averages near **−16°C**, **−85% sunlight** and **−68% precipitation**. | Four-crop calories fall about **90%** in Years 3–4; ocean NPP falls about **37%**. The food model estimates more than five billion famine deaths if no effective adaptation occurs. |

Primary sources: [Xia et al., *Nature Food* (2022)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43016-022-00573-0); [Jägermeyr et al., PNAS/NASA manuscript (2020)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200002122/downloads/20200002122.pdf); [Coupe et al., *Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres* (2019)](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2019JD030509); and [Toon et al., *Science Advances* (2019)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aay5478).

These values mix global means, crop-region means and maximum anomalies; they are labelled to prevent false comparison. A global mean temperature loss is not the temperature change on a British farm, and annual average sunlight does not capture a dark growing season.

### Ozone and ultraviolet light

The nuclear cases include a delayed agricultural stress not represented fully in most crop estimates. In one chemistry-climate simulation, 150 Tg of smoke produces peak global ozone loss of **75%**, with depletion lasting **15 years**. Elevated UV-B arrives as smoke clears, so it can damage crops during the apparent climatic recovery. A 2025 maize study estimates UV-B causes a further roughly **7%** severe-case loss, on top of cold and dimming. Sources: [Bardeen et al. (2021)](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2021JD035079) and [Shi et al., *Environmental Research Letters* (2025), summary](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/simulating-unthinkable-models-show-nuclear-winter-food-production-plunge).

## 4. The UK starting position

The scenario results need a baseline because a percentage production change is not an edible diet.

| Baseline feature | Latest evidence | Why it matters in an ASRS |
|---|---|---|
| Overall production-to-supply ratio | **60% for all food** and **72% for indigenous-type food** in 2025; 2024 values were 65% and 77% | These are value-based ratios, not calorie self-sufficiency, but they show structural import dependence |
| Fruit and vegetables | In 2024 domestic production covered about **15% of fresh fruit** and **53% of fresh vegetables** | Micronutrient-rich foods become scarce before raw calories necessarily do |
| Agricultural land | About **17 million ha** of utilised agricultural area in 2025, including **6.1 million ha croppable** and **3.0 million ha cereals** | Much UK agricultural land is grassland, not readily convertible to high-yield arable farming |
| Main arable assets | 2025: about **1.7 million ha wheat**, **1.1 million ha barley**, **241,000 ha oilseed rape** | Existing seed, machinery, mills and knowledge favour cereals and rapeseed |
| Feed competition | In 2023, **51.8%** of UK wheat, barley and oats was used as animal feed | Feed diversion can release calories quickly, but cannot replace imports or severe yield losses |
| Transport exposure | The Short Straits handled **18%** of food, feed and drink imports in the 2024 security assessment | Port, fuel, refrigeration and customs function may matter more than aggregate world output |

Sources: [Defra, *Agriculture in the United Kingdom 2025—food chain*](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/agriculture-in-the-united-kingdom-2025/chapter-14-the-food-chain), [UK Food Security Report 2024—supply sources](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/united-kingdom-food-security-report-2024/united-kingdom-food-security-report-2024-theme-2-uk-food-supply-sources), [UK agricultural land use 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/agricultural-land-use-in-the-united-kingdom/agricultural-land-use-in-united-kingdom-at-1-june-2025), and [UK Food Security Report 2024—supply-chain resilience](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/united-kingdom-food-security-report-2024/united-kingdom-food-security-report-2024-theme-3-food-supply-chain-resilience).

The all-food production-to-supply ratio counts value and includes exports. It must not be converted directly into “60% of UK calorie needs.” A proper ASRS balance sheet needs mass, edible energy, protein, fat and micronutrients by commodity, plus stocks, seed retention, losses and distribution.

## 5. Direct UK calorie results

The table below is the strongest country-specific numerical evidence found. It reports **Year-2 change in food calories relative to observed 2013 domestic production**, under a complete cessation of international trade. It comes from the preprint table corresponding to the later peer-reviewed Xia et al. analysis; the published paper gives the methods and global conclusions but not this full country table in its main text.

| UK Year-2 calorie change | 5 Tg | 16 Tg | 27 Tg | 37 Tg | 47 Tg | 150 Tg |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Livestock case** | **+0.7%** | **−32.7%** | **−43.8%** | **−62.1%** | **−78.5%** | **−95.8%** |
| **No-livestock case; 50% of food-competing feed eaten by people** | **+17.9%** | **−22.5%** | **−35.8%** | **−58.3%** | **−78.4%** | **−98.7%** |
| **Global livestock case, for comparison** | −8.1% | −23.7% | −33.2% | −42.0% | −49.0% | −81.1% |

Source: [Xia et al., “Global Famine after Nuclear War,” preprint Table 2](https://assets-eu.researchsquare.com/files/rs-830419/v1_covered.pdf?c=1660633629); peer-reviewed analysis: [*Nature Food* (2022)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43016-022-00573-0).

### What the table means—and what it does not

- **It is a production change, not population sufficiency.** A value of +0.7% says domestic production is slightly above its own 2013 level, not that Britain can feed everyone.
- **No trade is imposed, not predicted.** The exercise then scales national consumption to local production; it does not simulate ports, prices, export bans, bilateral agreements or humanitarian allocation.
- **Livestock dynamics are simplified.** Production is scaled with feed availability; the model does not represent slaughter timing, herd fertility, disease, cold housing or processing bottlenecks.
- **Only four crops are modelled explicitly.** Maize, rice, soybean and spring wheat responses are used to approximate other plant foods. Using spring wheat as a proxy is particularly important for the UK's large winter-wheat system.
- **The no-livestock case is not automatically better.** In the severe cases the feed crops themselves fail, while removing animal products removes calories derived from pasture and residues. Hence the UK result at 150 Tg is worse, not better.
- **Direct war damage is omitted.** In a severe war involving NATO and Russia, the UK could be attacked. The climate-food table does not remove bombed ports, refineries, fertiliser plants, mills, roads, farms or labour, nor does it include fallout on production land.

The figures are therefore best used as a **remote-climate-only conventional-food envelope**. For a nuclear event involving Britain, they are likely optimistic about executable production. For a volcanic or impact event with the same climate response but intact UK infrastructure, they may be closer to the relevant physical constraint.

## 6. Ingredient consequences by severity tier

### Tier 0 — Baseline

The current UK basket is broad because domestic cereals, meat, dairy, eggs, potatoes, sugar and oilseed are combined with imported fruit, vegetables, oils, rice, soy, cocoa, coffee, tea, spices and animal feed.

| Ingredient family | Baseline UK position | ASRS-relevant asset or vulnerability |
|---|---|---|
| Wheat, barley, oats | Major domestic production, established storage and milling/malting | Strongest scalable calorie base, but winter wheat and tall cereals may be vulnerable to low-light lodging |
| Potatoes and sugar beet | Established high-yield root crops | Useful carbohydrate sources; storage, disease control, harvesting energy and processing are essential |
| Rapeseed | Main domestic edible-oil crop | Fat is harder to replace than starch; seed crushing capacity is strategically important |
| Peas and field beans | Smaller domestic pulse sector | Human-edible protein; seed multiplication and processing capacity limit rapid expansion |
| Dairy, beef and sheep | Strong grassland base | Can use non-arable land and residues, but winter feed, veterinary inputs, cold chain and herd survival constrain output |
| Pigs, poultry and eggs | Efficient but reliant on cereals and imported protein feed | Contract rapidly when human food competes with feed |
| Fruit and vegetables | Heavily import-dependent, especially fruit | Micronutrient bottleneck and seasonal scarcity |
| Fish and seafood | Domestic landings plus imports; small calorie share | Valuable protein, fats and micronutrients, not a mass-calorie substitute |

For clean scenario design, add a **Baseline–trade failure control**: normal climate with ports and trade disrupted. This separates ASRS biology from wartime logistics and avoids attributing every missing ingredient to low sunlight.

### Tier 1 — Mild: 5 Tg

**Physical and production signal.** Global crop-region cooling peaks around 1.5°C in the Xia framework, with lower light and precipitation for several years. CLM5 gives a 7% average global four-crop calorie loss in Years 1–5; the independent multi-model crop comparison is nearer a 13% maize-and-wheat loss. The UK aggregate Year-2 food result is +0.7% in the livestock case and +17.9% with livestock removal/feed diversion.

**Most plausible UK ingredients:**

- wheat, spring barley, oats and rye, with earlier-maturing/cold-tolerant cultivars;
- potatoes and, where factories and fuel remain available, sugar beet;
- oilseed rape, though pollination, frost and low-light seed fill require testing;
- peas, field beans and lupins for protein;
- cabbage, kale, swede, turnip, carrot, leek and protected leafy greens;
- immediate meat from accelerated slaughter, followed by reduced herds;
- dairy from the best grass and conserved-feed systems; and
- managed fish and shellfish as nutrient supplements.

**Ingredients likely to become scarce first:** fresh imported fruit and vegetables; bananas and citrus; rice; soymeal; palm, sunflower and olive oils; coffee, cocoa, tea and spices; fresh poultry and pork once feed is redirected.

**Interpretation.** With functioning government, energy, seed distribution, rationing and some trade, the physical shock is probably manageable for Britain. With export bans and disrupted logistics, diversity and micronutrients can collapse despite near-baseline domestic calorie production. “Mild” is therefore a **rationing and substitution diet**, not business as usual.

### Tier 2 — Moderate: 16–47 Tg

This tier is deliberately broad. It should be plotted at 16, 27, 37 and 47 Tg rather than represented by one number.

| Moderate anchor | UK conventional calorie result, livestock case | No-livestock/feed-diversion case | Ingredient-system reading |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| **16 Tg** | −32.7% | −22.5% | Severe rationing; roots, grains, pulses and domestic oil can still form a recognisable diet |
| **27 Tg** | −43.8% | −35.8% | Outdoor crops become unreliable; fish declines 20–30% in many high-latitude waters; resilient-food ramp-up becomes important |
| **37 Tg** | −62.1% | −58.3% | Most countries lose 50–100% of modeled staple imports; UK availability depends heavily on stocks and industrial capacity |
| **47 Tg** | −78.5% | −78.4% | Conventional food cannot support the present population without major resilient-food production or imports |

**Outdoor crop ranking for Britain, as a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated yield forecast:**

| Relative prospect | Candidate ingredients | Reason | Principal uncertainty |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Better** | Spring barley, oats, rye; potato; swede/turnip; kale/cabbage; oilseed rape | Cool-season physiology, shorter cycles or storage-root/seed value | Extreme low light, frost at flowering, seed availability and mechanical harvest |
| **Conditional** | Spring wheat; sugar beet; peas; field beans; carrot, leek and spinach | Existing UK systems and useful carbohydrate/protein/micronutrient roles | Crop models use coarse proxies; factories, fertiliser and harvest energy may fail |
| **Poor outdoors** | Maize, soybean, most fruit, tomatoes/peppers, cucurbits | Warmth and/or light requirements; long season | A few southern or protected sites may remain productive in the lower moderate cases |
| **Technically possible under cover** | Potatoes, brassicas, leafy greens, mushrooms; some cereals or oilseeds | Temperature protection and controlled water | Transparent-film supply, structural snow/wind load, heat and supplemental lighting |

At 27–47 Tg, wheat should not be assumed to remain Britain's default staple merely because it dominates today. A 2026 growth-chamber preprint found that low-light temperate wheat lodged—stems collapsed—while canola performed better. Hand-harvested wheat grain was 0.6–2.8 t/ha, but machine-recoverable yield would be much lower. This is a small, unreplicated experiment, yet it identifies a failure mode absent from current crop models. Source: [Blouin et al., bioRxiv preprint (2026)](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.05.05.723012v1).

**Animal foods.** Poultry, eggs and pigs decline fastest because grain and soy become human food. Dairy contracts with grass and conserved-feed production; cattle and sheep retain a role on non-croppable grass, residues and by-products but herd rebuilding takes years. An initial meat glut from slaughter is followed by scarcity. Freezer capacity and electricity determine whether slaughter bridges months or merely creates waste.

**Fish.** Marine food is more climate-buffered than land crops, but not immune. Xia et al. model ocean NPP reductions from 3% at 5 Tg to 37% at 150 Tg; high-latitude fish catches fall about 20–30% around 27 Tg. Increased fishing can raise the first catch while depleting the stock, so fish cannot safely be treated as an unlimited emergency calorie source.

### Tier 3 — Severe: 150 Tg

**Physical and production signal.** The severe benchmark produces maximum crop-region cooling of 14.8°C and roughly 90% global four-crop loss in Years 3–4. The UK Year-2 conventional-food loss is 95.8% in the livestock case and 98.7% in the no-livestock/feed-diversion case. Outdoor agriculture in Britain is therefore a small and uncertain contributor.

**Likely sequence of UK ingredients:**

1. **Weeks to months:** existing flour, grain, oats, edible oil, sugar, pulses, canned food and frozen inventories; fresh meat and dairy from emergency slaughter; stored potatoes and roots; preserved fish.
2. **Months to Year 1:** severely rationed stocks; human use of suitable feed grain; mushrooms on prepared substrates; the earliest greenhouse or insulated-crop output; imported food from less-affected southern/tropical regions if shipping and diplomacy survive.
3. **Years 1–3:** lignocellulosic sugar from repurposed mills and biorefineries; methane single-cell protein; greenhouse crops; imported tropical seaweed; residual rapeseed, potatoes, canola and cold-tolerant crops where local conditions allow.
4. **Years 3–10:** more industrial food and relocated crops, then a gradual return of conventional cereals, roots, vegetables and livestock as climate and seed systems recover.

**Ingredient portfolio:**

| Function | Candidate ingredients | UK relevance | Critical dependency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bulk carbohydrate | Stored grain/sugar; lignocellulosic glucose; potatoes and residual roots | Britain has mills, breweries and paper/bioprocess assets worth inventorying | Energy, enzymes, sterile or food-safe processing, transport and feedstock |
| Protein | Methane single-cell protein; peas/beans; residual dairy/meat/fish; fungal biomass | Gas, fermentation and food-manufacturing skills could be strategic assets | Gas and electricity, oxygen, nitrogen, fermenters, skilled operators and approval |
| Fat | Rapeseed oil; animal fats; fish oil; possibly synthetic fat | A small mass of fat is nutritionally and calorically important | Oilseed survival and crushing; safety and scale of synthetic pathways |
| Vitamins/minerals | Brassicas and protected greens; potatoes; liver, dairy, eggs and fish; seaweed; supplements | Stocks and fortification can bridge crop failure | Vitamin stability, iodine control, cold chain and equitable distribution |
| Flavour/acceptability | Salt, acids, yeast, malt, herbs, fermented products | Matters for sustained adherence to monotonous emergency diets | Processing inputs and ration design |

For a nuclear severe case, these are optimistic technical possibilities. If the UK is a target, the same event that creates the soot can destroy exactly the ports, power, gas, chemical, fermentation and food-processing capacity required by the solution. A remote severe eruption or impact could produce the climate shock without that national industrial destruction; the food outcome would be very different.

## 7. Trade: not a switch, but the dominant branching assumption

Xia et al. impose zero international trade. Rivers et al. compare no-trade and maintained-trade optimisation. Jehn et al. model how yields alter an existing commodity network without assuming total closure. These are different questions.

The 2025 trade-network study uses a **37 Tg** scenario and wheat, maize, rice and soy, together representing around 60% of global food calories. It finds that most countries lose **50–100%** of their imports of those commodities because key northern exporters are severely affected. Australia becomes the only major wheat exporter in the model. The model omits export bans, conflict, shipping interruption and price responses, so it is a lower-bound physical disruption rather than a worst case. Source: [Jehn et al., *Earth System Dynamics* (2025)](https://esd.copernicus.org/articles/16/1585/2025/).

For Britain, three trade cases are more defensible than a binary open/closed assumption:

| Trade case | Assumption | UK consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Closed/war-damaged** | Ports, fuel, finance and agreements fail; exporters ban sales | Domestic stocks and production set a hard ceiling; tropical foods and resilient-food inputs largely disappear |
| **Constrained/reconfigured** | Shipping functions but northern exporters fail; trade shifts south | Australia and some tropical producers matter disproportionately; imports are smaller, expensive and politically allocated |
| **Maintained/cooperative** | Food, fuel and resilient-food inputs are globally optimised and shared | Technically best outcome, including severe-case survival in the ALLFED model; politically demanding and historically untested at this scale |

The maintained-trade case is a useful upper bound, not the default aftermath of a nuclear war.

## 8. What the ALLFED adaptation literature actually shows

ALLFED-affiliated studies are most valuable as **technical feasibility and capacity estimates**. They do not show that governments, firms or households would deploy the technologies at the specified speed.

| Intervention | Published estimate | Earliest useful tier | UK reading | Evidence limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Feed diversion and livestock reduction** | At 5 Tg, UK Year-2 change improves from +0.7% to +17.9%; at 27 Tg, from −43.8% to −35.8%; almost no benefit at 47 Tg | Mild–moderate | Immediate, low-technology calorie release; must retain seed and feed for useful animals | Stylised 50% diversion; not a herd-transition model |
| **Cool-tolerant crops and relocation** | A 2025 preprint finds worst-year global calorie loss of about 23%, 53% and 85% at 16, 47 and 150 Tg without adaptation; relocation could double severe-case output but still leaves a large deficit | Moderate–severe | Spring grains, rapeseed, roots and brassicas; UK may lose advantage as production shifts toward lower latitudes | Preprint; assumes rapid seed and land reallocation |
| **Low-tech tropical greenhouses** | Modelled scale reaches 36% of global food requirements after 12 months and all requirements after about 30 months | Moderate–severe | More likely an import source than UK-local severe-case production; British winter light remains low | Assumes huge polymer-film and construction mobilisation, tropical sites and functioning trade |
| **Lignocellulosic sugar** | Repurposed pulp/paper and related plants could begin in about 5 months and supply ~28% of global calorie needs within Year 1; estimated retail cost US$0.82/kg | Severe | Strong candidate bulk carbohydrate if UK industry and enzymes survive | No emergency conversion has occurred at this scale; sugar supplies no protein, essential fat or most micronutrients |
| **Methane single-cell protein** | With 24/7 construction, 7–11% of global protein needs at end of Year 1; all protein in 2.5–4.5 years; estimated US$3–5/kg dry | Severe | Potential UK protein source linked to gas and chemical/fermentation capability | Very high capital, gas, electricity, oxygen, nitrogen and skilled-labour requirements |
| **Tropical seaweed** | Modelled scale equivalent to 45% of global food demand in 9–14 months, using a small ocean area | Severe | Primarily an import/feed/biofuel input; UK local severe-case growth was not demonstrated | Human intake limited by iodine; farm materials, drying and shipping are enormous requirements |
| **Food stocks and rationing** | Published integrated models consume stocks in Year 1 and shift calories between years | All tiers | Fastest bridge; high confidence as a concept | Actual UK public/private stocks, ownership, location and spoilage are not publicly resolved at ingredient level |

Sources: [Rivers et al., *Global Food Security* (2024)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2211912424000695); [Blouin et al., crop-relocation preprint (2025)](https://eartharxiv.org/repository/view/10178/); [Alvarado et al., *Science of the Total Environment* (2020)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0048969719360085); [Throup et al., *Food and Bioproducts Processing* (2022)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960308521001620); [García Martínez et al., *Frontiers in Bioengineering and Biotechnology* (2022)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2022.906704/full); and [Hinge et al., *Aquaculture International* (2025)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10499-025-01978-x).

### Integrated-model result

Rivers et al. model the **150 Tg** case using stored food, conventional crops, livestock, fisheries, cool-tolerant crops, methane single-cell protein, lignocellulosic sugar, greenhouses and seaweed. With no trade and no adaptation, global famine results. Rapid resilient-food deployment mitigates the shock for many countries; with maintained global trade, the optimisation can feed the global population.

That last statement is often compressed into “ALLFED shows everyone can be fed.” The defensible formulation is narrower: **a linear optimisation model finds a technically sufficient portfolio under assumptions of continued industrial operation, rapid construction, information sharing, international allocation and trade**. It is a capacity upper bound, not a behavioural forecast.

## 9. Nutrition is a separate constraint

Calories alone do not define an edible or healthy ingredient system. Pham et al. test diets across a stored-food/livestock phase, a production-trough phase and a scaled resilient-food phase. Their portfolios can meet many macronutrient and micronutrient targets, but recurring weaknesses include **vitamins D, E and K and calcium**; some phases also risk shortfalls in vitamins A and C, niacin, pantothenic acid, zinc, iodine and selenium. Source: [Pham et al., *Nutrients* (2022)](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/14/3/492).

### UK ingredient responses to likely nutritional bottlenecks

| Nutritional need | Candidate sources | ASRS problem |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | Grains, roots, sugar, oils | Severe tier removes nearly all outdoor supply; pure sugar diets are nutritionally incomplete |
| Protein | Pulses, microbial/fungal protein, dairy, meat, fish | Industrial protein scales slowly; animal protein collapses after the slaughter phase |
| Essential fats / vitamin E | Rapeseed, other oilseeds, fish, stored oils | Oilseed yield and storage oxidation; low-fat emergency portfolios |
| Calcium | Dairy, small fish with bones, some greens, fortified food | Dairy declines; green output is small; fortification salts need stockpiling |
| Vitamin D | Oily fish, eggs, fortified foods or supplements | Weak sunlight and limited animal foods; supplements are the reliable bridge |
| Vitamin K and folate | Brassicas and leafy greens | Protected-crop area may be prioritised for these rather than bulk calories |
| Vitamin C | Potatoes, cabbage/kale, stored or greenhouse vegetables | Long storage and cooking reduce content |
| Iodine | Iodised salt, fish, seaweed | Seaweed can oversupply iodine; species and processing control are essential |
| B12 | Animal foods, microbial production, supplements | Plant-heavy emergency diet requires deliberate fortification |

Pre-event stocks of vitamin/mineral premix, iodised salt and stable supplements are unusually high-leverage because they require far less mass and space than calorie stockpiles.

## 10. Nuclear, volcanic and impact cases should not share one narrative

| Trigger | Published analogue | Approximate duration and signal | Additional damage | How to use in the tier system |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nuclear smoke** | 5–150 Tg soot experiments | Roughly a decade; strongest crop effects first 2–4 years; ozone can remain depleted longer | Blast, fire, fallout, electromagnetic and infrastructure effects; war and hostile trade | Primary numerical tier spine; add a separate direct-damage case |
| **Large volcanic eruption** | Pinatubo-scale events are observed; super-eruptions remain model-dependent | Usually shorter sulfate forcing, often 1–3 years for ordinary major eruptions | Ash and regional infrastructure damage; no necessary global war | Match by surface light/temperature/duration, not aerosol mass; likely bridging-stock and 1–3 harvest problem unless exceptionally large |
| **Bennu-type asteroid** | 100–400 Tg impact dust in a 2025 model | Worst case about **−4°C**, **−15% precipitation**, 20–30% photosynthesis loss and 3–4 years of disturbance | Crater-region destruction, tsunami/fire depending on impact site; ozone loss about 32% | Roughly a moderate global physical analogue, but not equivalent to 27 or 37 Tg nuclear soot |

Sources: [USGS, “Volcanoes Can Affect Climate”](https://www.usgs.gov/programs/VHP/volcanoes-can-affect-climate) and [Dai et al., “Climatic and ecological responses to Bennu-type asteroid impact,” *Science Advances* (2025)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adq5399).

Even this mapping is coarse. The Bennu study models dust-driven terrestrial NPP, not UK crop yields or the same food basket. A super-eruption's particle size, sulfur yield, latitude and season can move it across tiers. The 150 Tg nuclear case is severe but not a physical upper bound for a larger impact.

## 12. Two response cases to cross with every tier

### Unprepared / fragmented

- little public knowledge of food stocks or industrial conversion plans;
- rapid export bans and weak international allocation;
- damaged ports, fuel and electricity systems;
- no pre-positioned cold-tolerant seed or supplement stocks;
- livestock slaughter exceeds freezer and processing capacity;
- market access and physical distribution are unequal; and
- resilient-food construction begins only after the event.

### Prepared / cooperative

- calorie, protein, fat and micronutrient stocks mapped by location;
- rationing and feed-diversion triggers agreed in advance;
- seed kits and multiplication plans for spring grains, rapeseed, roots, pulses and brassicas;
- protected-crop film, enzymes, nutrients, fermenter designs and critical spare parts stockpiled;
- priority power/fuel for mills, cold stores, fertiliser, water, ports and fermentation;
- standing trade and shipping agreements with southern producers; and
- fortification and food-safety rules written before the emergency.

This produces an eight-cell core design:

| Physical tier | Unprepared / fragmented | Prepared / cooperative |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | Current system plus ordinary shocks | More resilient current system |
| Mild | Import and dietary crisis despite manageable domestic crops | Rationing and crop adjustment; broad survival diet |
| Moderate | Famine risk, particularly above 27–37 Tg | Narrow conventional diet plus rapidly scaling resilient foods |
| Severe | Near-total conventional failure and likely mass mortality | Survival technically possible in models, but dependent on intact industry and trade |

## 13. Model audit: the main reasons not to overstate confidence

1. **Soot production is the largest upstream uncertainty.** Warhead number does not determine soot without target selection, fuel loading, ignition, fire spread, plume dynamics and removal.
2. **The evidence is not fully independent.** Several crop, fish, trade and food-optimisation papers use climate output from the same CESM-WACCM nuclear-war simulations. Many downstream papers do not constitute new climate replications.
3. **Conventional-crop coverage is narrow.** Four crops represent much of the global calorie system; UK winter wheat, barley, oats, potatoes, sugar beet, rapeseed, brassicas and grass need direct simulation and experiments.
4. **Low light may create unmodelled crop failures.** The 2026 growth-chamber lodging result is a warning that extrapolating ordinary crop models far outside calibration can fail.
5. **Percent production is not food access.** Stocks, seed, waste, processing, fuel, prices, ration entitlement and local distribution determine consumption.
6. **Adaptation studies are feasibility analyses.** They often assume 24/7 construction, intact specialised labour, rapid regulatory decisions and reallocation of global industrial capacity.
7. **Direct nuclear damage is missing from the climate-food numbers.** This is especially consequential for the UK in the 150 Tg NATO–Russia scenario.
8. **Trade assumptions bracket reality rather than predict it.** Zero trade and optimally maintained global trade are useful bounds; neither is a central forecast.
9. **The event season matters.** A 15 May soot injection interacts with Northern Hemisphere crops differently from an autumn or winter event.
10. **Recovery of climate is not recovery of agriculture.** Lost herds, orchards, seed systems, machinery, soil fertility, fisheries and institutions have longer memory.

The National Academies recommends coordinated model intercomparison across the entire causal chain. Until such work exists, numerical precision should not be mistaken for narrow uncertainty.

## 14. Data gaps most worth filling for a UK paper

| Priority | Missing evidence | Why it changes the ingredient result |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | UK gridded daily light, temperature, frost, precipitation and soil conditions for all six soot cases | Converts global scenario labels into planting and harvest windows |
| 2 | UK crop simulations and growth-chamber trials for winter/spring wheat, barley, oats, rye, potato, sugar beet, rapeseed, peas, beans, kale and swede | Replaces global four-crop proxies with British ingredients |
| 3 | UK edible-energy balance by commodity, including imports, exports, feed, seed, losses and stocks | Determines whether +0.7% production change is sufficient or still a deficit |
| 4 | Month-by-month private and public food stocks by location and ownership | Establishes the real bridging period |
| 5 | Livestock transition model with slaughter, cold storage, pasture, feed, fertility and veterinary constraints | Resolves the short meat surplus and later protein/fat deficit |
| 6 | Port, electricity, gas, fertiliser, milling, cold-chain and fuel dependencies | Tests whether theoretical food can be produced and moved |
| 7 | UK conversion audit for paper mills, breweries, biorefineries and fermentation plants | Grounds lignocellulosic sugar and microbial protein in actual assets |

## 15. Recommended parameter set for subsequent scenario building

For each tier, record the following rather than just a severity name:

| Parameter block | Variables |
|---|---|
| **Forcing** | aerosol type and mass; injection latitude, altitude, month; UK surface shortwave/PAR; temperature; precipitation; frost-free days; duration |
| **Conventional food** | crop-specific yield; grass/pasture; livestock feed and slaughter; fish catch; protected cropping |
| **Food balance** | beginning stocks; imports/exports; feed diversion; seed retention; processing loss; household waste; ration kcal/protein/fat |
| **Infrastructure** | electricity, gas, liquid fuel, fertiliser, ports, roads, mills, cold storage, water, communications and labour availability |
| **Adaptation** | cold-tolerant seed; crop relocation; greenhouse area; lignocellulosic sugar; SCP; seaweed; mushrooms; fortification |
| **Distribution** | trade regime; ration policy; price control; regional inequality; priority groups; humanitarian transfers |

For narrative clarity, the following anchors are recommended:

- **Baseline:** present UK system, plus an optional normal-climate trade-failure control;
- **Mild:** 5 Tg, constrained trade, rationing and feed diversion;
- **Moderate-low:** 16 Tg;
- **Moderate-central:** 27 Tg for crop/fish analysis;
- **Moderate-upper:** 37 Tg for trade analysis, with 47 Tg as the near-collapse sensitivity;
- **Severe:** 150 Tg, reported twice—once with UK infrastructure intact and once with direct nuclear damage.

## Bottom line for the paper

The published evidence supports a credible tiered ingredient story, but not a single deterministic menu.

- **Baseline:** today's diverse domestic-plus-import system.
- **Mild:** recognisable British staples with rationing, feed diversion, lower animal output and much less imported diversity.
- **Moderate:** a narrow roots–small-grains–rapeseed–pulses–brassicas system, supplemented increasingly by protected and industrial foods; famine risk rises sharply above 27–37 Tg.
- **Severe:** stored food and livestock slaughter provide only a bridge; conventional UK agriculture contributes little, and the viable portfolio becomes industrial carbohydrate and protein, micronutrient fortification, protected crops and imports from residual productive regions.

The most important modelling choice is not whether an ingredient is theoretically growable. It is whether the UK retains the **energy, machinery, seed, processing, ports, labour and political allocation system** needed to turn biological production into an accessible diet.
